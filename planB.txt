]EARLY WARNING SYSTEM FOR SCHOOL DROPOUT (SOUTH AFRICA)
Full Presentation & Project Notes
=========================================================


1. THE PROBLEM
---------------------------------------------------------
About 40% of learners who start Grade 1 in South Africa
never make it to Grade 12. They disappear from the school
system somewhere along the way.
(Source: DG Murray Trust's Zero Dropout Campaign)

Meaning: out of every 100 kids who start Grade 1, about 40
of them are gone before matric.

Research points to Grade 10, 11, and 12 as the years where
the steepest drop-off happens (nationally, only ~84% move
from Grade 10 to 11, and ~78% move from Grade 11 to 12).
We are using this existing research, not claiming we
discovered it ourselves — we have not yet built our own
model to prove this number independently.

The real gap: there is no widely deployed, data-driven tool
that predicts, ahead of time, WHICH schools are at highest
risk of losing learners — so districts and NGOs currently
find out mostly after the fact, when it's too late to help.

Note: this is NOT about matric pass rates or exam results.
We are not arguing with Umalusi or the Department of Basic
Education's numbers. Pass rate and dropout rate measure two
different things. Our focus is only on learners leaving the
system before they even get to write the exam.


2. OUR SOLUTION
---------------------------------------------------------
A model that predicts, per school, the expected dropout %
for its current Grade 10, 11, and 12 cohorts — using a
school's own history (enrollment trends, funding level,
teacher-to-learner ratio, past pass-rate trend) — so
districts and support organizations know WHICH schools to
help FIRST, BEFORE learners actually leave.

Example output:
  "School X is predicted to lose 25% of its Grade 10 class
   before reaching matric."

In one line:
"We're not trying to fix the whole education system. We're
trying to give people a warning light — so they know WHICH
schools to help first, and BEFORE the damage is already done."


3. WHERE THE DATA COMES FROM (concrete, real sources)
---------------------------------------------------------
This is the part that makes the project buildable, not just
an idea. Here is exactly where the data lives:

a) DBE EMIS - Schools Masterlist
   Source: Department of Basic Education, EMIS unit
   Access via: education.gov.za (EMIS section) and the
   Open Data South Africa toolkit's education page
   What it gives: every registered school in the country,
   with a unique EMIS number, school name, phase (primary/
   secondary), physical address, district, and province.
   Why we need it: this EMIS number becomes the KEY that
   lets us join every other dataset together, school by
   school, year by year.

b) DBE "No Fee Schools" lists (published per province, per
   year, as PDFs)
   Example confirmed: "Western Cape No Fee Schools 2023" —
   contains EMIS number, school name, phase, full address,
   education district, QUINTILE (funding level, 1 to 5),
   and LEARNER NUMBERS for that year, for every school in
   that province.
   Why we need it: this gives us funding quintile AND a
   year-by-year learner headcount per school — repeat this
   for multiple years (e.g. 2019, 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023)
   and we can calculate enrollment TRENDS per school, which
   is one of our most important features.

c) SNAP Survey of Ordinary Schools (annual, national)
   Source: DBE EMIS directorate
   Hosted by: DataFirst (University of Cape Town),
   datafirst.uct.ac.za — also referenced on the World Bank
   microdata catalog
   Coverage confirmed: 1997-2016 (version 1.6), collected
   from every ordinary school in the country each year
   What it gives: learner and educator numbers per school,
   staff category (educators, support staff, admin staff),
   full-time/part-time status, gender
   Why we need it: this is how we calculate the TEACHER-TO-
   LEARNER RATIO per school per year — a known predictor of
   how well-resourced a school is.
   Limitation to flag honestly: the confirmed public dataset
   runs up to 2016. More recent years likely exist inside
   DBE's internal EMIS system but are not confirmed as
   openly downloadable — this is something we'd need to
   verify directly with DBE or DataFirst, and is a real
   constraint on how current our data can be.

d) National Senior Certificate (NSC) Examination results,
   by school
   Source: DBE, distributed via DataFirst
   Coverage confirmed: 2010-2016 microdata (registration
   required through DataFirst's Data Access Agreement
   process); more recent years are published as annual PDF
   "School Performance Reports" and "School Subject Reports"
   directly by DBE and by provincial education departments
   (e.g. Western Cape Education Department publishes its own
   NSC award/results lists)
   What it gives: pass rate and subject performance, by
   school, by year
   Why we need it: pass-rate TREND per school is one of our
   model's core features, and (longer-term) it is also how
   we would validate whether our dropout-risk predictions
   line up with real subsequent outcomes.

e) Stats SA - General Household Survey (GHS)
   Source: Statistics South Africa
   Access via: Stats SA's own data portal / Nesstar system,
   and referenced on data.gov.za
   What it gives: household-level survey data including
   school attendance status and REASONS for not attending
   school (e.g. no money, pregnancy, illness, distance),
   broken down by province
   Why we need it: this is the only data source that tells
   us WHY learners leave, at a province level — useful for
   explaining model results and for choosing which kind of
   support (financial, psychosocial, transport) fits a
   region's actual driving factors.

f) Provincial open data portals
   Example confirmed: Western Cape Government (WCG) Open
   Data Portal, wcg-opendataportal-westerncapegov.hub.arcgis.com
   What it gives: provincial-level datasets, some with GIS/
   mapping layers
   Why we need it: useful for adding geography (rural vs.
   urban, distance-based features) and for a first pilot
   province, since Western Cape's data appears to be some
   of the most complete and well-organised publicly.

g) DG Murray Trust / Zero Dropout Campaign published research
   Source: dgmt.co.za and zerodropout.co.za
   What it gives: the national dropout statistics we are
   quoting (the ~40% figure), plus their own case studies on
   Early Warning Systems already piloted with NGO partners
   Why we need it: this is our evidence that the problem is
   real and already a national priority, and it is the
   benchmark we can sanity-check our own numbers against.

Summary — one sentence per source:
  EMIS Masterlist        -> school identity & join key
  No Fee Schools lists   -> quintile + yearly learner counts
  SNAP Survey            -> teacher/educator numbers per school
  NSC results by school  -> pass-rate trend per school
  Stats SA GHS           -> province-level reasons for leaving
  WCG Open Data Portal   -> provincial pilot data + geography
  Zero Dropout Campaign  -> national benchmark statistics


4. THE FULL DATA PROCESS (step by step)
---------------------------------------------------------
STEP 1 - Collect
  Download the datasets above, starting with ONE province
  (Western Cape looks most complete) and a handful of years
  (e.g. 2016-2023) to keep the first version manageable.

STEP 2 - Clean and join
  Use the EMIS number as the common key to join:
    School Masterlist + No Fee Schools list (quintile,
    learner counts) + SNAP survey (staff numbers) + NSC
    results (pass rate) — all into one table, one row per
    school per year.
  Handle problems: schools that closed, merged, or changed
  EMIS numbers; missing years; independent (non-no-fee)
  schools that may not appear in the No Fee list (these
  would need a separate source, e.g. the full EMIS Masterlist
  learner counts, if available).

STEP 3 - Engineer features (the inputs the model actually uses)
  For each school, for each year, calculate:
    - Grade 10 enrollment this year vs. Grade 9 enrollment
      last year (a proxy signal for repeaters/early leavers)
    - Grade 11 enrollment this year vs. Grade 10 enrollment
      last year (tracks the cohort forward one year)
    - Grade 12 enrollment this year vs. Grade 11 enrollment
      last year
    - Funding quintile (1-5, or no-fee flag)
    - Teacher-to-learner ratio (from SNAP)
    - Pass-rate trend over the last 3 years (from NSC results)
    - District and province (as categories)
    - Rural vs. urban flag

STEP 4 - Build the label (what we're trying to predict)
  For a school's Grade 10 cohort in year Y, track that same
  school's enrollment through Grade 11 (year Y+1) and Grade 12
  (year Y+2), and matric candidates (year Y+2 or later,
  allowing for repeaters).
  Dropout % (our label) = 1 minus (learners who made it to
  matric-writing stage / learners who started Grade 10 that
  year), for each historical cohort where we already know
  what happened.

STEP 5 - Split the data
  Train on older cohorts (e.g. Grade 10 classes from
  2016-2020, whose outcomes we already know) and hold back
  the most recent known cohort (e.g. 2021 or 2022) purely for
  testing — this is how we check if the model's predictions
  are actually trustworthy before trusting it on this year's
  live Grade 10 class.

STEP 6 - Train the model
  Start simple: Linear Regression (easiest to explain to a
  non-technical audience: "each feature adds or subtracts
  some % risk").
  Then try a stronger model: Random Forest Regressor or
  Gradient Boosting (e.g. XGBoost) — these handle
  non-straight-line relationships better (for example, being
  very underfunded AND very short-staffed together might be
  much worse than either problem alone, and tree-based models
  capture that kind of interaction).
  Compare both — if the stronger model isn't meaningfully
  better, the simpler, more explainable one is the better
  real-world choice.

STEP 7 - Evaluate
  Check Mean Absolute Error (MAE): on average, how many
  percentage points off are our predictions from the real
  historical dropout %?
  Check R-squared: how much of the difference between
  "high-risk schools" and "low-risk schools" does our model
  actually explain?
  Look at which features matter most (feature importance) so
  we can explain WHY a school is flagged, not just THAT it's
  flagged.

STEP 8 - Deploy / present
  Grist: hold the school-level table (school, district, %
  predicted, key driving factor) as a live, shareable database.
  Flourish: build a ranked bar chart or a choropleth map
  colouring districts/schools by predicted dropout risk, for
  the actual presentation and for any decision-maker to browse.


5. WHY THIS APPROACH, IN SIMPLE TERMS
---------------------------------------------------------
- Grade 10-12 is where research shows the steepest drop, so
  that's where our model focuses.
- We predict at the SCHOOL level, not the individual learner
  level, because individual learner records are private and
  protected by law (POPIA) — we don't have legal access to
  named student data. School-level data is public.
- The output is a PERCENTAGE per school, not a yes/no per
  student — this makes it a resource-ranking tool for
  decision-makers, not a surveillance tool on individual kids.
- This isn't a made-up idea. The Zero Dropout Campaign
  already told the Department of Basic Education that an
  Early Warning System is needed, and already runs a manual,
  spreadsheet-based, individual-level version inside specific
  schools (a red/amber/green "traffic light" system). Our
  school/district-level model is the missing macro layer
  above that — it tells a province WHICH schools to prioritise,
  not just which individual kid inside one school to watch.


6. BUSINESS MODEL (how this survives past the hackathon)
---------------------------------------------------------

1) THE PROBLEM (brief)
  ~40% of learners who start Grade 1 never finish Grade 12,
  worst between Grade 10-12. No existing open, data-driven
  tool predicts ahead of time WHICH schools are at highest
  risk, so help can be sent before it's needed rather than
  after learners have already left.

2) THE SOLUTION
  A model that predicts each school's expected dropout % for
  its Grade 10-12 cohorts, using open historical school data
  (enrollment trends, funding quintile, teacher-learner
  ratio, pass-rate trend), and ranks schools/districts so
  decision-makers know where to act first — delivered as a
  live Grist database feeding a Flourish dashboard.

3) WHO IT'S FOR — the customer / beneficiary, clearly split:
  USERS (who operate the tool):
    - Department of Basic Education (national) and provincial
      education departments — resource allocation, and finally
      tracking dropout as a measurable KPI
    - NGOs already in this exact space (Zero Dropout Campaign,
      Masibumbane Development Organisation) — a scaled-up
      replacement for their manual Excel traffic-light system
    - District officials and school principals — a ranked,
      actionable list instead of guesswork
  BENEFICIARIES (who the value ultimately reaches):
    - The learners and families who get support before
      dropping out, instead of being counted only afterward

4) HOW IT DELIVERS VALUE — why it's better than what exists:
    - Existing tool: a manual Excel spreadsheet with a simple
      red/amber/green rule-of-thumb, run school-by-school,
      by whichever NGO happens to be present in that school.
    - Our tool: a trained statistical model, run at
      district/province scale, using multiple real factors at
      once (not just one rule), so it can rank ALL schools in
      a province against each other — something no one
      currently has a way to do.
    - It turns "we think dropout is a problem here" into "this
      specific school is projected to lose 25% of its Grade 10
      class, and here's the main factor driving that number,"
      which is what actually justifies where money and staff
      get sent.

5) HOW IT'S FUNDED / SUSTAINED
    - Not sold to learners, families, or schools — it is a
      tool FOR government/NGOs, not a product they buy from us.
    - It plugs into an ecosystem that already funds this exact
      problem: DG Murray Trust funds the Zero Dropout Campaign
      already; provincial education departments already have
      EMIS/data budgets.
    - Realistic funding paths: (a) grant funding from an
      existing education-focused funder (DGMT or similar),
      (b) adoption/commissioning by a provincial education
      department as a planning tool, (c) partnership with an
      NGO that absorbs it into their existing program funding.
    - It does not need a subscription model to survive — its
      sustainability comes from being useful enough that an
      existing funded body wants to keep running it.

6) HOW IT SCALES
    Step 1: Pilot in ONE province with the most complete open
    data (Western Cape looks strongest based on our research).
    Step 2: Validate predictions against that province's own
    real historical outcomes (does a school we flagged as
    high-risk actually show a bigger drop the following year?).
    Step 3: Expand province by province, re-checking that the
    same features still predict well in each new province
    (funding/context differs a lot between provinces).
    Step 4 (long-term/national): propose integration with
    SA-SAMS and LURITS — the exact national learner-tracking
    system DBE has already been asked to build by the Zero
    Dropout Campaign — so the model eventually runs on live,
    national data instead of separately-collected provincial
    files.

7) TEAM / RESOURCES — what's actually needed to build and run it:
    DATA ACCESS:
      - The open datasets in Section 3 (EMIS Masterlist, No
        Fee Schools lists, SNAP survey, NSC results by school,
        Stats SA GHS)
      - Ideally, direct contact with DataFirst (for registered
        microdata access) and/or a provincial education
        department (for the most current data, since some
        public datasets stop around 2016)
    PARTNERSHIPS:
      - A conversation with the Zero Dropout Campaign / DGMT
        would be extremely valuable — they already have real
        outcome data and credibility with DBE, and could
        sanity-check our model against ground truth
    PEOPLE / SKILLS NEEDED ON THE TEAM:
      - Someone to collect and clean the data (spreadsheet/
        Python work: joining tables by EMIS number, handling
        missing years)
      - Someone to build and test the model (Python, e.g.
        pandas + scikit-learn for linear regression / random
        forest / gradient boosting)
      - Someone to build the presentation layer (Grist for the
        live data table, Flourish for the map/chart dashboard)
      - Someone to handle the pitch/business case (this
        document, the "why this matters" framing for judges or
        a real-world partner)
    TECH STACK (all free/low-cost, usable within a hackathon):
      - Python (pandas, scikit-learn) or a spreadsheet tool for
        modelling
      - Grist for the structured, shareable database
      - Flourish for the public-facing visualization
      - No paid infrastructure required to build a working
        prototype


7. IMPORTANT LIMITATIONS TO BE HONEST ABOUT
---------------------------------------------------------
- We predict at the SCHOOL level, not the individual learner
  level — we don't have access to private, named student data.
- Some of our confirmed public datasets (SNAP survey, NSC
  microdata) are only clearly confirmed open up to around
  2016, meaning recent years may need to be sourced from
  annual PDF reports instead of clean downloadable files —
  this adds manual work we should plan for honestly.
- The model won't be perfect:
    - False positive: a flagged school doesn't lose as many
      learners as predicted — low cost, just extra support
      sent that wasn't strictly needed.
    - False negative: an unflagged school still loses
      learners we didn't predict — the real risk, and why the
      model needs regular retraining and monitoring.
- "Grade 10-12 is the steepest" is based on existing external
  research, not a number we've independently proven ourselves
  from our own data yet — our model, once built, is what lets
  us check this claim against real numbers.
